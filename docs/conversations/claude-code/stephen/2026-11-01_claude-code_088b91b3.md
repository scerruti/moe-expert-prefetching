**Claude:**

Yes, and **the math explains the pattern, though the √*k* part is suggestive rather than proven.** The argument has three steps:

**1. Spacing.** The log-probability gap between neighbouring ranks is about *s*(*k*) ≈ α/*k*, with α ≈ 0.5. The fit gives a slope of −1.00, and the relation holds up to about *k* = 16.

**2. Change rate (well supported).** A swap happens when the noise δ exceeds the local gap, so:

> rate ≈ E|δ| / *s* ∝ *k*

Noise is nearly flat across ranks, so this predicts the fitted rate of about 0.48% × *k*. Noise ÷ spacing also matches the measured rate to within a steady factor of about 0.7 where changes are rare.

**3. Buffer (the √*k* part).** Needing a buffer greater than *m* means δ > *m* · *s*. If the noise has a heavy tail, P(δ > *x*) ≈ *A* · *x*^(−β), then the share of changes needing more than *m* is:

> P(δ > *m* · *s*) / rate ∝ *m*^(−β) · *s*^(1−β)

Setting that equal to 1 − *q* and solving gives:

> ***m* ∝ *k*^(1 − 1/β) · (1 − *q*)^(−1/β)**

The intuition is that larger *k* lets smaller disturbances cause flips, so the typical flip needs relatively less buffer. With light-tailed noise, *m* would grow almost in proportion to *k*. **β = 2 gives exactly √*k*.** A heavy tail also makes sense here: most rows have noise of about 0.002, while rows downstream of a flip have about 0.024, and that mixture produces one.

**Testing it:**

| Check | Result | Implied β |
|---|---|---|
| Tail measured directly, 95th–99.9th percentile | 1.72–1.84 | **about 1.8** |
| Tail measured directly, top 1% | 2.31–2.49 | **about 2.4** |
| Growth of *m*95 − 1 with *k* (slope 0.55) | close to 0.5 | **about 2.2** |
| Ratio of (*m*99 − 1) to (*m*95 − 1), which should be 5^(1/β), at *k* = 4–16 | 2.10–2.46 | **about 1.8–2.2** |
